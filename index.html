Where on the ocean does one DDM pixel look?

Every cell of a delay–Doppler map collects power from the sea surface where an iso-range ellipse crosses an iso-Doppler hyperbola. Point at a pixel on the DDM and its footprint lights up on the surface — usually in two places at once.

Why an ellipse and a hyperbola

Points at equal total path length T→surface→R lie on an ellipsoid with the two satellites as foci; where it meets the sea it draws an ellipse around the specular point. Points sharing a range-rate draw hyperbola-like curves set mainly by the receiver’s velocity — the transmitter is 20 000 km away, so it barely tilts them.

The two-fold ambiguity

An ellipse and a hyperbola cross twice. A single DDM cell therefore sums power from two patches on opposite sides of the specular point, and no amount of processing can tell them apart from one measurement. Only at zero delay do the two merge into the specular blob.

Reading the horseshoe

The bright arch is the support boundary, not a feature of the sea. The delay-τ ring has a half-width in Doppler proportional to √τ, so no surface point can produce a large Doppler at small delay — the empty region above the arch is geometrically forbidden.

Model. Flat-plane bistatic geometry in a specular-centred ENU frame; delay (|r−Rᴛ| + |Rᵣ−r| − d₀)/c in L1 C/A chips (1 chip = 293.05 m), Doppler (ûᴛ·vᴛ − ûᵣ·vᵣ)/λ referenced to the specular point. Surface power uses Zavorotny–Voronovich geometric optics, σ⁰ = π|ℜ|²(q/qᵧ)⁴P(−q⊥/qᵧ), with a Gaussian slope PDF and Katzberg-scaled Cox–Munk mean-square slope. The DDM is formed by binning the surface integral and convolving with |Λ(Δτ)|²·|sinc(Δf·Tᵢ)|² for Tᵢ = 1 ms, so the map and the highlighted footprints come from exactly the same computation. Earth curvature, antenna pattern and receiver noise are omitted.
